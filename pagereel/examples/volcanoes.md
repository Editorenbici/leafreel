# How Volcanoes Work

Deep below our feet, Earth's outer shell is broken into huge slabs called tectonic plates. They move very slowly, only a few centimeters a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Most volcanoes form where these plates pull apart or crash into each other. Others, like the islands of Hawaii, sit above hot spots, places where hot rock rises from deep inside the planet.

Rock that melts underground is called magma. Magma is lighter than the solid rock around it, so it slowly rises and collects in a space called a magma chamber. Magma contains dissolved gases, mostly water vapor and carbon dioxide. As the magma climbs, the pressure drops and the gas forms bubbles, a bit like the fizz in a bottle of soda when you open it.

When the pressure becomes too strong, magma pushes out through a crack or an opening called a vent. This is an eruption. Once magma reaches the surface it is called lava, and it can be hotter than 700 degrees Celsius. Some volcanoes pour out runny lava that flows gently for kilometers. Others explode and throw ash, rocks and gas high into the sky.

Scientists describe two common shapes. Shield volcanoes are wide and gently sloped because their lava is runny. Stratovolcanoes are tall and steep cones built by many layers of lava and ash. Many of the world's active volcanoes are found around the Pacific Ocean, in a belt known as the Ring of Fire.

Volcanoes are dangerous, but they also give us gifts. Volcanic ash breaks down into very fertile soil, so farmers have grown crops on volcanic slopes for thousands of years. In some countries, heat from underground is used to make electricity and to warm homes.

To stay safe, volcano scientists called volcanologists watch for warning signs. They measure small earthquakes, changes in the gas coming out of the ground, and tiny swelling of the mountain. When the signs grow, authorities can tell families to leave before an eruption. Every community near a volcano should have an evacuation plan and a meeting point that everyone knows.
